There was once a watchmaker who spent his days building and repairing clocks. He enjoyed solving difficult problems, understanding intricate mechanisms, and finding ways to make things work that hadn’t worked before. He also spent much of his time questioning how clocks ought to be made, exploring new approaches, and sharing his ideas with fellow watchmakers.

But the watchmaker had a peculiar habit.

He often forgot to wind the clock in his own workshop. His letters went unanswered for days. He sometimes missed appointments. And although he could spend hours studying the movement of tiny gears or contemplating the future of watchmaking, he occasionally forgot to write down the simplest instructions.

The other watchmakers found this infuriating.

“How can someone who understands something so complicated fail at something so simple?” they asked.

One day, the king summoned the watchmaker.

{{< message side="left" color="watercolor-rose" >}}
Your work is extraordinary. Not only do you build remarkable clocks, but your ideas have changed how others think about watchmaking. Yet your carelessness is unacceptable. If you can imagine clocks that nobody has built before, surely you can remember to wind the one on your wall.
{{< /message >}}

The watchmaker thought for a moment.

{{< message side="right" color="watercolor-blue" >}}
Your Majesty, you are right. The clock should be wound. The letters should be answered. And I must find better ways to ensure these things happen.
{{< /message >}}

{{< message side="left" color="watercolor-rose" >}}
Then why don't you simply do them?
{{< /message >}}

{{< message side="right" color="watercolor-blue" >}}
Because, Your Majesty, the way my mind works makes some things easier and others surprisingly difficult. I have spent my life learning to work with that mind, but I cannot make it into somebody else's.
{{< /message >}}

The king considered this.

{{< message side="left" color="watercolor-rose" >}}
So what do you propose?
{{< /message >}}

The watchmaker fell silent. Then he remembered that they were living in the age of artificial intelligence.

{{< message side="right" color="watercolor-blue" >}}
Perhaps I have been trying to solve the wrong problem all along.
{{< /message >}}

He returned to his workshop and built himself an AI clerk. One that kept track of his appointments, reminded him of unanswered letters, and made sure the clocks were wound.

And something remarkable happened.

The workshop became more reliable. The letters were answered. The appointments were kept. And the watchmaker, no longer spending so much of his energy fighting his own nature, could devote more attention to building clocks, exploring new ideas, and sharing what he learned with others.

The age of AI gives us an opportunity: instead of spending our lives trying to become equally good at everything, we can build systems that compensate for our limitations and allow us to make better use of our strengths.

Perhaps the goal was never to become good at everything, but to find a way to make everything work together.
